📖 Opening Theory

Reference notes on individual openings — move order, plans, and traps. ♞ King's Gambit — as White

1.e4 e5 2.f4 (ECO C30–C39). White offers the f-pawn to pull Black's e-pawn away from the center, open the f-file for a rook once castled, and aim straight at f7 — the square only Black's king defends. It's the most aggressive way to meet 1...e5, at the cost of White's own king safety and a permanent pawn deficit if the attack doesn't land.

Move order & the major branches

After 2.f4, Black's two main replies are accepting the pawn (2...exf4, the King's Gambit Accepted) or declining it. Each branch below is collapsed by default — click to expand a line.

King's Gambit Accepted — 2...exf4

3.Nf3 g5 — Classical Variation, main tabiya
1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 g5

Black defends the extra f4-pawn immediately. This is the starting point for most of the sharpest King's Gambit theory. White's main tries from here are 4.h4 (attacking the g5-pawn's defender) and 4.Bc4 (ignoring it and developing toward f7).

4.h4 g4 5.Ne5 — Kieseritzky Gambit, the main line of the whole opening.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 g5 4.h4 g4 5.Ne5

The most-analyzed line in King's Gambit theory. After 5...Nf6 (Berlin Defense), the main continuation runs 6.Bc4 d5 7.exd5 Bd6 8.d4 Nh5 9.O-O Qxh4 10.Qe1 Qxe1 11.Rxe1, reaching a well-known, roughly equal position (the Anderssen Defense line) with play for both sides.

6.Bc4 d5 7.exd5 Bd6 8.d4 Nh5 9.O-O Qxh4 10.Qe1 Qxe1 11.Rxe1

⚡ Trap for White

If Black deviates with 6...Bg7 instead of 6...d5, White gets a free pawn and the initiative: 7.Bxf7+! Kf8 8.Bb3 — Black's king is stuck in the center, unable to castle, for no compensation.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 g5 4.h4 g4 5.Ne5 Nf6 6.Bc4 Bg7 7.Bxf7+ Kf8 8.Bb3

4.h4 g4 5.Ng5?! is the Allgaier Gambit — a piece sacrifice generally considered unsound by modern engines, though still dangerous over the board against an unprepared opponent.

♟ Notable game — Spassky vs Fischer, Mar del Plata 1960

The Kieseritzky Gambit's most historically important game: Spassky beat a 17-year-old Fischer with it, and the loss stung badly enough that Fischer went on to invent the Fischer Defense (3...d6, see below) specifically to avoid ever facing it again — then wrote his famous 1961 essay "A Bust to the King's Gambit" off the back of it. Full game on chessgames.com ↗

4.Bc4 g4 5.O-O — Muzio Gambit

One of the most theoretically extreme lines in chess: White sacrifices a whole knight, not just a pawn, for a mating attack.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 g5 4.Bc4 g4 5.O-O gxf3 6.Qxf3

Instead of retreating the attacked knight, White castles and lets Black capture it. After 6.Qxf3, White is down a full piece but has three attackers (queen, bishop, and soon a rook) converging on f7, with Black's king still in the center and no time to consolidate. It requires very precise defense from Black — a single slip and the attack is often decisive within 15 moves. Not a line to play without preparation on both sides of the resulting sacrifice.

♟ Notable game — Anderssen vs Zukertort, Breslau 1865 ("When the Muzio Stopped")

Adolf Anderssen — the same player as the Immortal Game below — crushes Johannes Zukertort's defense in one of the most cited demonstrations of the Muzio's attacking potential; Black resigned facing an unstoppable mating net around move 17. Full game on chessgames.com ↗

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 g5 4.Bc4 g4 5.O-O Qe7 6.Nc3 gxf3 7.d4 d6 8.Nd5 Qd7 9.Qxf3 Nc6 10.Qxf4 Nd8 11.Qg3 c6 12.Qxg8 Rxg8 13.Nf6+ Ke7 14.Nxg8+ Ke8 15.Nf6+ Ke7 16.Nxd7 Bxd7 17.Bg5+ 1-0
4.Nc3 g4 5.Ne5 — Quaade Gambit
1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 g5 4.Nc3

A quieter try than the Kieseritzky/Muzio family. White's main idea is to challenge the f4-pawn with g2-g3 while keeping the c3-knight ready to jump to d5.

⚡ Trap for White

If Black grabs material too greedily after the g3 break, White's queen breaks through decisively:

4...g4 5.Ne5 Qh4+ 6.g3 fxg3 7.Qxg4 g2+ 8.Qxh4 gxh1=Q 9.Qh5!

Black has won a rook and promoted a pawn, but is completely undeveloped with the queen about to be lost to threats around the king — White is winning.

3...d6 — Fischer Defense
1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 d6

Named for Bobby Fischer's approach from his essay "A Bust to the King's Gambit" — a patient waiting move rather than the committal 3...g5. Play often continues 4.d4 g5 5.h4 g4 6.Ng1, an unusual-looking position where neither side develops a piece for several moves, or 4.Bc4, which is played at least as often in practice.

⚡ Trap for White

A classic error for Black is repeating the Classical Variation's ...g5 push here without the same tactical justification (historically associated with a Morphy game, Morphy–Tilghman, 1859):

4.Bc4 g5? 5.h4! g4 6.Ng5 Nh6 7.d4 f6 8.Bxf4!

White ignores the attack on the g5-knight and instead recaptures the f4-pawn with a large advantage — Black's kingside is full of holes and the g5-knight, Bc4, and newly active dark-squared bishop are all bearing down on a king that can't easily get safe.

3...Nf6 — Schallopp Defense
1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 Nf6

Develops immediately and defends f4 indirectly via a later ...Nh5. Seen at the top level as recently as the 2020 Magnus Carlsen Invitational.

4.e5 Nh5

⚡ Trap for White

The h5-knight is Black's one real weakness in this line — misplaying the follow-up loses it outright:

5.d4 d6 6.Qe2 Be7? 7.exd6 Qxd6 8.Qb5+

The check forces Black to block or move the king, and once it's parried, White's queen swings back to h5 and simply wins the trapped knight. Black's correct try instead is 6...d5, keeping the position balanced.

Other Accepted lines: 3...h6, 3...d5, 3...Be7

Becker Defense — 3...h6, preparing to build the pawn chain h6–g5–f4 without ever needing ...g4. Usually met with 4.d4 g5, transposing back toward Classical/Fischer-type positions.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 h6 4.d4 g5

Modern (Abbazia) Defense — 3...d5, striking back in the center at once rather than defending the f4-pawn.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 d5 4.exd5 Nf6

The main try, the Botvinnik Variation, continues 5.Bb5+ c6 6.dxc6 bxc6 7.Bc4 Nd5, when Black has reasonable central compensation for the pawn.

5.Bb5+ c6 6.dxc6 bxc6 7.Bc4 Nd5

Cunningham Defense — 3...Be7, with the immediate point of ...Bh4+ disrupting White's castling.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 Be7 4.Bc4 Bh4+ 5.Kf1

White's king loses castling rights but the position stays roughly balanced with chances for both sides.

3.Bc4 — Bishop's Gambit

White develops immediately instead of playing 3.Nf3, accepting that Black gets a free tempo with check.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Bc4 Qh4+ 4.Kf1

3...Qh4+ is close to forced for Black here — the real point of 3.Bc4 is that without the check, White's simple follow-up Qh5 (threatening Qxf7#, since only the king guards that square) is a serious problem, so Black essentially has to spend a tempo on the check to stay safe rather than developing naturally.

A quieter, well-regarded try for Black is 3...d5, striking back in the center immediately:

3...d5 4.Bxd5 Nf6 5.Nc3 Bb4 6.Nf3 Bxc3 7.dxc3 c6 8.Bc4 Qxd1+ 9.Kxd1 O-O 10.Bxf4 Nxe4

This line (the Bledow Variation) simplifies into a roughly equal position after mutual queen trades and recaptures.

♟ "The Immortal Game" — Anderssen vs Kieseritzky, London 1851

The single most famous game in chess history, played during a break at the first international tournament. Adolf Anderssen sacrifices both rooks and a bishop, then delivers checkmate with just his three remaining minor pieces while his queen and a rook sit untouched on their home squares. The name was coined afterward by fellow player Ernst Falkbeer — the same Falkbeer whose Counter-Gambit appears below. Full game & annotations on Chess.com ↗

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Bc4 Qh4+ 4.Kf1 b5 5.Bxb5 Nf6 6.Nf3 Qh6 7.d3 Nh5 8.Nh4 Qg5 9.Nf5 c6 10.g4 Nf6 11.Rg1 cxb5 12.h4 Qg6 13.h5 Qg5 14.Qf3 Ng8 15.Bxf4 Qf6 16.Nc3 Bc5 17.Nd5 Qxb2 18.Bd6 Bxg1 19.e5 Qxa1+ 20.Ke2 Na6 21.Nxg7+ Kd8 22.Qf6+ Nxf6 23.Be7# 1-0

Fittingly, Bobby Fischer — whose own brush with the Bishop's Gambit's sister line cost him the Spassky game above — later took up 3.Bc4 himself as White and won all three recorded tournament games where he played it.

King's Gambit Declined

2...d5 — Falkbeer Counter-Gambit

Rather than accepting, Black strikes back in the center at once. Named for 19th-century Austrian master Ernst Falkbeer.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 d5 3.exd5 e4

The traditional main line — Black sacrifices a pawn back for rapid development and a strong e4-pawn cramping White. It fell out of favor after the 1930s once 4.d3! was established as a solid antidote for White, undermining the e4-pawn immediately.

A more modern try is the Nimzowitsch Countergambit, 2...d5 3.exd5 c6 (also employed by Frank Marshall), intending to recapture on c6 with a piece rather than committing the e-pawn forward:

3...c6 4.Nc3 exf4 5.Nf3 Bd6 6.d4 Ne7 7.dxc6 Nbxc6

♟ Notable game — Anderssen vs Falkbeer, Berlin 1851

The founding, eponymous game for this whole opening — Ernst Falkbeer, playing Black against Anderssen himself (the same Anderssen from the Immortal Game above), demonstrates the counter-gambit's dynamic potential, eventually promoting a second queen and mating the White king in the open center.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 d5 3.exd5 e4 4.Bb5+ Bd7 5.Qe2 Nf6 6.Nc3 Bc5 7.Nxe4 O-O 8.Bxd7 Nbxd7 9.d3 Nxd5 10.Nf3 Re8 11.f5 Bb4+ 12.Kf2 N7f6 13.g3 Qd7 14.c4 Nxe4+ 15.dxe4 Nf6 16.e5 Qxf5 17.Kg2 Rad8 18.a3 Bd6 19.Rd1 Qh5 20.c5 Rxe5 21.Qxe5 Qg4 22.cxd6 Re8 23.Qxe8+ Nxe8 24.d7 Qe4 25.d8=Q Qc2+ 26.Bd2 0-1

2...Bc5 — Classical Declined

The bishop develops actively, eyeing f2 and discouraging White from castling short too casually.

1.e4 e5 2.f4 Bc5 3.Nf3 d6

White most often follows up with 4.c3 (preparing d4), 4.Bc4, or 4.Nc3 — ordinary development rather than the sharp gambit-compensation themes of the Accepted lines, since Black hasn't taken the pawn.

🚫 A trap White must avoid, not spring

This is the reverse of the traps above — a mistake for White to steer clear of. Grabbing the e5-pawn immediately is a serious error:

3.fxe5?? Qh4+ 4.Ke2?? Qxe4#

Checkmate — verified move by move: the queen check forces the king forward, and after the blundering 4.Ke2, every escape square is covered (d1/d2/f1 blocked by White's own pieces, d3/e1/f3 all attacked by the queen once it captures on e4). White must instead block with 4.g3, when 4...Qxe4+ is still uncomfortable but not immediately fatal. The lesson: don't play 3.fxe5 at all — it walks into exactly the tempo-losing check that 3.Nf3 or 3.Bc4 are designed to meet safely.

A note on the game citations above: a specific, verified historical game is linked for the Classical/Kieseritzky, Muzio, Bishop's Gambit, and Falkbeer lines. The Quaade, Fischer Defense, Schallopp, Becker/Modern/Cunningham, and Classical Declined branches don't have an equally famous named game attached — rather than link something unverified, those sections stick to the trap/theory content above.

Middlegame plans for White

Across most of the Accepted lines, White's compensation for the pawn rests on the same handful of ideas:

  • The open f-file. Once castled, a rook on f1 stares straight down at f7 — the one square only Black's king defends in the opening. Much of King's Gambit theory is about forcing Black to either weaken that square further or fall behind in development trying to hold it.
  • The central pawn duo. A follow-up d2-d4 (often paired with recapturing the gambit pawn via Bxf4) gives White a full e4/d4 center and lasting space, even in lines where the direct kingside attack doesn't land.
  • Development speed over material. Nf3, Bc4, O-O, and sometimes d4 all come before White worries about the missing pawn — the whole opening is a bet that piece activity outweighs it.
  • The tradeoff: king safety. f2 is permanently weaker after 2.f4, and ...Qh4+ (or ...Bh4+/...Be7-h4+ in some lines) is a recurring resource for Black that can cost White castling rights. Most named traps above exploit White's willingness to accept this risk — and Black's failure to.

Against the Declined lines (Falkbeer, Classical Declined), these gambit-compensation themes don't apply the same way since Black hasn't taken the pawn — plans there look more like ordinary open-game development, with White's main technical task being to neutralize Black's active bishop/central pawn rather than to attack f7.

Endgame themes

The King's Gambit is built around a middlegame attack, not a favorable endgame — there isn't a body of King's Gambit "endgame theory" the way there is for, say, a Ruy Lopez structure. If the direct attack is defused and material stays level or White remains a pawn down, the compensation that carries into an endgame is mostly the same positional edge as the middlegame: better development, a more active king (once safely tucked away), and better outposts for the knights, particularly one that's already reached e5 or g5 in the Kieseritzky/Muzio-family lines. In practice, a King's Gambit player who hasn't converted the attack by the middlegame is usually playing to hold a slightly worse but resourceful position, not to win a technical endgame.

♟ Ruy Lopez, Schliemann Defense — as Black

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 f5 (ECO C63). Rather than meet the Ruy Lopez with a quiet main line, Black immediately counter-attacks the e4-pawn, accepting long-term risk to the kingside pawn structure and king safety for early piece activity and open lines. It's the most combative reply to 3.Bb5 — "positionally risky, especially against a strong and prepared opponent," in Wikipedia's phrasing, but a real practical weapon: Teimour Radjabov used it against the world's elite from 2008–11, scoring +1 −0 =10 against opponents including Carlsen, Anand, Topalov and Karjakin.

A naming footnote: English-language sources call this the Schliemann Defense after 19th-century player Adolf Schliemann, but the opening was actually first analyzed by the Finnish-Russian theorist Carl Jaenisch in 1847 — Schliemann's own published analysis was of a different line entirely (3...Bc5 4.c3 f5). Russian and German sources correctly call it the Jaenisch Gambit; the misattribution just stuck in English.

Move order & the major branches

After 3...f5, White's four main tries are 4.Nc3 (sharpest, requires the most preparation), 4.d3 (quieter, now White's most popular choice), 4.Bxc6 (the Exchange Variation), and 4.exf5 (grabbing the pawn, generally considered White's weakest try). Each branch below is collapsed by default — click to expand a line.

4.Nc3 fxe4 5.Nxe4 Nf6 — today's main line
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 f5 4.Nc3 fxe4 5.Nxe4 Nf6

Black develops with tempo, forcing matters immediately. 5...Nf6 has overtaken the older 5...d5 (below) as Black's main choice — it's more solid and less likely to lose outright to a single inaccuracy.

6.Nxf6+ Qxf6 7.Qe2 Be7 8.Bxc6 dxc6 9.Nxe5

White ends up a pawn ahead, but Black has the bishop pair and open lines for the rooks — this pawn sacrifice for activity is the whole point of the Schliemann, not a side effect of it.

4.Nc3 fxe4 5.Nxe4 d5 — the traditional sharp main line
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 f5 4.Nc3 fxe4 5.Nxe4 d5

The older main line and still the most razor-sharp: Black strikes the center at once rather than developing first. It produces some of the most forcing, well-mapped theory in the whole opening — and also some of the most dangerous for Black, since a single wrong move here can lose quickly. Modern theory rates it as slightly better for White with best play, which is why 5...Nf6 above has become the safer practical choice.

6.Nxe5 dxe4 7.Nxc6 Qg5 8.Qe2 Nf6 9.f4 Qxf4 10.Ne5+ c6 11.d4 Qh4+ 12.g3 Qh3 13.Bc4 Be6

10.Ne5+ is a discovered check — the knight steps off the b5-e8 diagonal and reveals an attack from White's bishop, still sitting on b5 since move 3. The resulting position (⩲, a slight White edge per standard theory) is a well-known drawish-but-playable endgame-ish structure that both sides have to know well entering the line.

4.d3 — White's modern main try

Simply defending e4 rather than entering the sharpest theory. It has overtaken 4.Nc3 in popularity in recent years precisely because it sidesteps Black's most-analyzed resources.

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 f5 4.d3 fxe4 5.dxe4 Nf6 6.O-O Bc5 7.Bxc6 bxc6 8.Nxe5 O-O 9.Nc3 d6

Black offers the e5-pawn back for quick development and open lines toward White's king, rather than trying to hold everything. This is a critical tabiya both sides need to know.

4.Bxc6 — Exchange Variation

White trades off the bishop rather than let Black define the tension. Black usually recaptures toward the center.

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 f5 4.Bxc6 dxc6

White's calmest follow-up develops normally rather than grabbing material: 5.Nc3 Nf6 6.Qe2 Bc5 7.exf5 Qe7, heading for a roughly balanced position with mutual chances.

5.Nc3 Nf6 6.Qe2 Bc5 7.exf5 Qe7

⚡ Trap for Black

If White instead grabs the e5-pawn immediately, Black has a strong reply that hits two pieces at once:

5.Nxe5?! Qd4! 6.Nf3 Qxe4+

5...Qd4 attacks both the loose knight on e5 and the e4-pawn simultaneously; White can't save both, and 6...Qxe4+ regains the pawn with an active, centralized queen and the bishop pair to boot. It gets much worse for White if the natural-looking check is tried instead:

6.Qh5+?? g6 7.Nxg6 hxg6 8.Qxg6+ Kd8

The point of 6.Qh5+?? is to fork g6 and h8 hoping to win the exchange, but Black's queen on d4 defends h8 along the long diagonal (d4–h8) the whole time, so the rook was never really hanging. Engine-checked material after 8...Kd8 comes out exactly level (33 points a side by standard piece values), but Black keeps the bishop pair against White's lone remaining minor piece and has a far more comfortable position — the king move to d8 costs castling rights, but it's a small price for the piece activity and structure Black gets in return.

4.exf5 — Gambit Accepted, and 4.d4

Considered White's weakest try — grabbing the f-pawn hands Black the center for free.

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 f5 4.exf5 e4 5.Qe2 Qe7 6.Bxc6 dxc6 7.Nd4 Nh6

Black already stands better here — White's extra pawn on f5 is more of a long-term weakness than an asset, and Black's development and central space are worth more.

⚡ Trap for Black

4.d4!? tries to meet the center head-on before Black can build the same structure, but it runs into a clean tactical refutation:

4.d4 fxe4 5.Nxe5 Nxe5 6.dxe5 c6!

The point of 6...c6 is that White's bishop on b5 can't move — stepping away allows ...Qa5+ followed by ...Qxe5, regaining the pawn with a fine position. White has to shed the extra e5-pawn back and Black equalizes comfortably.

The Exchange Variation (4.Bxc6) and Gambit Accepted (4.exf5) branches don't have an equally famous named game attached — rather than link something unverified, those sections stick to the theory and trap content above.

Middlegame plans for Black

Across the Accepted-pawn-back lines, Black's compensation rests on the same handful of ideas:

  • The open f-file and e-file. Once the center opens up, Black's rooks get active quickly, often before White's own pieces are fully coordinated.
  • The bishop pair. Most main lines see White trade a bishop for a knight (via Bxc6) — Black's two bishops, once developed, are a long-term asset even in structurally messy positions.
  • Central and kingside activity over material. Black is frequently a pawn down for real, structural reasons — the whole opening is a bet that piece activity and White's slightly loosened kingside outweigh it, not an accident to be corrected.
  • The tradeoff: king safety and structure. Black's own king often can't castle short comfortably and the kingside pawns get damaged in several lines (the g6/h-pawn structures above). Black is trading long-term structural soundness for short-term activity — a bet that has to pay off before the middlegame ends, or the weaknesses start to matter more than the activity.

Endgame themes

Like the King's Gambit, the Schliemann is fundamentally a middlegame weapon, not an endgame-favoring structure — Black's whole strategic bet is to generate enough activity that the game is decided well before an endgame is reached. When it does reach an endgame (as several of the games above do, including two long ones), the deciding factor is usually whichever structural damage has proven more costly: White's slightly exposed king and awkward piece placement from the early complications, versus Black's weakened kingside pawns and the pawn deficit in lines where material was never won back. Both games above that Black won were decided in long rook-and-minor-piece endgames, not quick kingside attacks — a reminder that even in a sharp opening built around activity, technique in the resulting endgame still matters.

♘ Caro-Kann, Two Knights Attack — as White

1.e4 c6 2.Nc3 d5 3.Nf3 (ECO B10–B11). Rather than the main-line 2.d4, White develops both knights before committing the d-pawn at all — a flexible, less-theoretical way to meet the Caro-Kann that keeps options open between d3 and d4 depending on how Black sets up. It was a favorite of a young Bobby Fischer before falling out of top-level fashion; a well-known trap buried in one of its main tries has caught two former World Champions.

Move order & the major branches

After 3.Nf3, Black's most common tries are 3...Bg4 (pinning the knight immediately), 3...dxe4 (resolving the center at once), 3...Nf6, and 3...d4 (chasing the knight away). Each branch below is collapsed by default — click to expand a line.

3...Bg4 — the Mindeno Variation, main tabiya
1.e4 c6 2.Nc3 d5 3.Nf3 Bg4

Named for Dutch player Siegfried van Mindeno, on the losing end of an instructive 1927 example of the line (below). White's near-universal reply challenges the bishop immediately:

4.h3

Black has two genuinely different replies from here, each with its own name in the databases.

4...Bxf3 5.Qxf3 — Mindeno Variation, Exchange Line, the main theoretical line.

1.e4 c6 2.Nc3 d5 3.Nf3 Bg4 4.h3 Bxf3 5.Qxf3 e6

Black hands over the bishop pair for simpler development and a solid structure — considered roughly equal by standard theory. White's follow-ups include 6.d4, 6.g3 (fianchetto), and 6.Be2, all aiming to use the extra space and the pressure the queen already exerts on d5/f6 to keep some initiative without real risk.

4...Bh5 5.g4 — Mindeno Variation, Retreat Line, sharper: White grabs kingside space at once instead of trading off the bishop.

1.e4 c6 2.Nc3 d5 3.Nf3 Bg4 4.h3 Bh5 5.g4

Committal on both sides — White's kingside pawns are now permanently loosened, but the bishop on h5 is shut out of the game unless Black can round it up with a timely ...e6 and ...Nd7-f8-e6 rerouting.

3...dxe4 4.Nxe4 Nf6 — the solid main continuation
1.e4 c6 2.Nc3 d5 3.Nf3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 Nf6 5.Qe2 Nxe4 6.Qxe4 Nd7 7.d4

Black resolves the center immediately and trades down to a quiet, roughly equal position — solid rather than ambitious. From 6.Qxe4, Black has reasonable alternatives too: 6...Be6 (developing before recapturing the tempo lost to the queen) or 6...Nd7 followed by 7.Bc4 Nf6 8.Ne5 e6, both leading to similarly balanced middlegames.

3...dxe4 4.Nxe4 Bf5?! — the classical-transposition trap

Instead of 4...Nf6, developing the bishop first looks natural — it's the standard idea from the Classical Caro-Kann (1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 Bf5). But without White's d-pawn already committed to d4, the resulting position is subtly different, and one specific continuation has trapped two world-class players historically.

1.e4 c6 2.Nc3 d5 3.Nf3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 Bf5 5.Ng3 Bg6 6.h4 h6 7.Ne5 Bh7 8.Qh5 g6 9.Bc4 e6 10.Qe2

⚡ Trap for White

Two natural-looking replies both lose on the spot to the same shot, 11.Nxf7! — the knight sacrifice rips open Black's king since 9...gxh5 was already ruled out by 10.Bxf7#, and the king has nowhere safe to hide once f7 falls.

3...Nf6 4.e5 — the Advance-like try
1.e4 c6 2.Nc3 d5 3.Nf3 Nf6 4.e5 Ne4 5.Ne2 Qb6 6.d4 e6 7.Ng3 c5

White gains space with the early e5 push; Black counters in the center with ...c5. The point comes with a small but sharp tactical justification for White's setup:

8.Bd3 Nxg3 9.fxg3!

Recapturing away from the center looks strange, but it opens the f-file for a rook and gives White a real kingside attacking chance — standard theory rates the resulting position as slightly better for White (⩲). This exact system faded from top-level practice after a run of a few years, but it's still a real practical try below the very top.

3...d4 — chasing the knight away
1.e4 c6 2.Nc3 d5 3.Nf3 d4 4.Ne2 c5

Immediately gains space and kicks the c3-knight, at the cost of giving White a free hand in the center later (Black's ...d4 isn't backed up by a timely ...e5 the way it would need to be to fully justify the space grab). White's main tries from here are 5.c3, striking back at the pawn chain immediately, or 5.Ng3, simply rerouting the knight to a good square.

5.c3 Nc6 6.cxd4 cxd4 7.Qa4

The straightforward 5.c3 line tends to leave Black somewhat worse — White gets the freer position and a slightly better version of an isolated/passed d-pawn structure.

Middlegame plans for White

  • Flexible d-pawn timing. The whole point of delaying d4 is choosing between d3 (solid, keeps options open, matches the modern main lines) and d4 (more space, more theory) based on what Black actually plays — there's no single "right" central structure the way there is in the main-line Caro-Kann.
  • Punishing the bishop trade. When Black trades on f3 with ...Bxf3, White's doubled or shifted pawns are a long-term consideration, but the bishop pair and the extra tempo the recapturing queen gains are usually worth more in practice, especially with quick central and kingside expansion.
  • The kingside pawn storm. h4-g4(-g5) ideas recur constantly — in the Retreat Line to trap the bishop on h5, and in the classical-transposition lines to hunt down the bishop on g6/h7 before Black can castle. Several of the sharpest lines above are built entirely around this plan.
  • Development speed. Two knights out before the bishop or the d-pawn is the whole identity of the system — White is consistently willing to spend a move on a slightly unusual recapture (9.fxg3!, 6.Qxe4) to keep pieces active rather than worrying about structural purity.

Endgame themes

As with the King's Gambit and the Schliemann, there's no dedicated "Two Knights Attack endgame theory" — the system doesn't steer toward a particular favorable endgame structure the way an isolated-queen's-pawn or minority-attack opening might. What it does produce, repeatedly in the games above, is long technical fights: both Fischer–Petrosian (68 moves) and Keres–Tartakower (50 moves) were decided well into deep middlegame-to-endgame territory, not by an early tactical knockout. The trap line is the one clear exception — when it lands, it's over in 11-12 moves. Outside of that specific trap, White's practical winning chances come from the same long-game factors as any other Caro-Kann structure: whichever side's minor pieces end up better placed once the position simplifies.

♙ Caro-Kann, Accelerated Panov Attack — as White

1.e4 c6 2.c4 (ECO B10). White plays c4 on move two, before touching the d-pawn at all — a move-order trick aimed straight at the heart of the Caro-Kann. The threat is simple: if Black plays the natural 2...d5, White meets it with 3.exd5 cxd5 4.d4, transposing directly into the well-known Panov-Botvinnik Attack (normally reached via 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.exd5 cxd5 4.c4) — but a move faster, and with an extra independent try available along the way that the main-line move order doesn't offer.

Move order & the major branches

After 2.c4, Black's replies split into three very different categories: playing 2...d5 as normal (by far the most common, but branching further depending on how Black recaptures), the rare 2...e5 counter-strike, or simply transposing into an ordinary Caro-Kann with a quieter second move. Each branch below is collapsed by default — click to expand a line.

2...d5 3.exd5 cxd5 4.d4 — transposing to the Panov-Botvinnik Attack
1.e4 c6 2.c4 d5 3.exd5 cxd5 4.d4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 6.Nf3 Be7

This is the whole point of the "accelerated" move order in its most straightforward form: White simply reaches the standard Panov-Botvinnik Attack's main tabiya — the classic isolated-queen's-pawn (IQP) middlegame, White's d4-pawn unsupported by another pawn but compensated for by faster development and control of e5 — one tempo sooner than 2.d4 would allow. Everything known about the regular Panov-Botvinnik Attack's middlegame plans applies from here.

2...d5 3.cxd5 cxd5 4.exd5 Nf6 — the Modern Variation

Rather than transposing, White can capture with the c-pawn first, offering an independent structure the classical 2.d4 move order can't reach directly. Both capture orders (3.cxd5 first, or 3.exd5 first) lead to the same position and are both filed under the same name in the databases.

1.e4 c6 2.c4 d5 3.cxd5 cxd5 4.exd5 Nf6

Black doesn't rush to recapture the pawn on d5, developing with tempo instead. White's main tries from here are 5.Nc3, simply defending the extra pawn and developing, and 5.Qa4+, adding an extra check before doing the same thing.

5.Nc3 Nxd5

Material is level again once the knight recaptures, in an open, roughly symmetrical position — very different in character from the IQP middlegames of the transposition line above, despite both starting from the same 2.c4.

2...d5 3.exd5 Qxd5?? — the pseudo-Scandinavian trap

After 3.exd5, recapturing with the queen looks completely natural — it's exactly what happens in the real Scandinavian Defense (1.e4 d5 2.exd5 Qxd5). But White's early c4 changes everything.

1.e4 c6 2.c4 d5 3.exd5 Qxd5

⚡ Trap for White

Unlike the real Scandinavian, White already has a pawn on c4 here — and c4 attacks d5 directly. The queen isn't just inconvenienced by a developing check the way it is in a normal Scandinavian; it's simply hanging.

4.cxd5

Engine-checked: this is a genuine, ordinary pawn capture that wins the queen outright for a pawn, not a tactic with any hidden point Black could be relying on. The correct recapture is 3...cxd5 instead, transposing into the lines above — this trap only exists because ...Qxd5 is the automatic, memorized reply in the real Scandinavian, and here that same reflex simply loses.

2...e5 — the Open Variation
1.e4 c6 2.c4 e5 3.Nf3

A rare, ambitious try that turns the game into something resembling a normal 1.e4 e5 open game — except Black can't defend the e5-pawn the standard way with 3...Nc6, since Black's own c-pawn is already sitting on c6. Black has to find another way to hold the center (3...d6, 3...Qe7, or simply developing and accepting some central tension), and White's extra tempo from not having played an early ...Nc6-blocking move for Black tends to translate into a small, lasting edge.

Middlegame plans for White

  • Choosing your structure, not just your opening. The whole point of 2.c4 is that White decides, move by move, whether to steer toward the well-trodden IQP middlegames of the standard Panov (rich in theory, well understood by both sides) or the more independent, less-charted Modern Variation structures — a real practical weapon precisely because Black has to be ready for both.
  • The IQP compensation package, when it arises. In the transposition line, the same ideas as any Panov-Botvinnik or Isolated-Queen's-Pawn middlegame apply: piece activity and outposts (especially e5) outweigh the long-term structural weakness, provided White keeps pieces on and doesn't drift into a pure endgame where the isolated pawn becomes a simple target.
  • Punishing move-order carelessness. The pseudo-Scandinavian trap is the clearest example, but the whole opening is built around Black needing to recognize that this isn't quite the position their memorized Caro-Kann or Scandinavian theory assumes.
  • Development speed over structural symmetry. In the Modern Variation especially, material and structure end up level fast — White's edge, when there is one, comes from being a touch faster to develop and castle, not from a long-term pawn-structure advantage the way the IQP lines provide.

Endgame themes

The two faces of this opening lead to genuinely different endgame outlooks. In the transposition line, the classic Panov-Botvinnik IQP question applies directly: White's isolated d-pawn is a long-term target once queens and enough minor pieces come off, so White generally wants to avoid over-simplifying and keep enough pieces on the board for the pawn's dynamic benefits (space, outposts, open lines) to matter. In the Modern Variation, both games above ended in fairly level, simplified endgames (a drawn rook ending in Vaganian–Dreev, a balanced middlegame-to-endgame draw in Radjabov–Azmaiparashvili) — a fair reflection of a structure that's closer to equal than the IQP lines, where technique and small edges matter more than any built-in structural advantage.
