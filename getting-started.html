🏆 How to Get Started as a Tournament Player

A short guide to your first rated tournament: getting a US Chess (USCF) ID, finding events near you, and finding a local club to train with. Written with Portland, OR as the worked example — the national steps apply anywhere.

1 Create a US Chess (USCF) membership ID

US Chess (the national governing body — formerly "USCF") issues every rated player a unique member ID. You need an active membership to play in a US Chess–rated tournament; without one, your results won't count toward an official rating.

  • Go to US Chess's join page and fill in your name, date of birth, and address.
  • Membership is tiered by age (scholastic, youth, adult) — the join form picks the right option for you and shows the current fee before you pay.
  • Your member ID appears immediately after checkout — write it down, it's what tournament directors will ask for at registration.
  • New to the process? US Chess's own Information for New Members page covers what to expect at your first event.

💰 Money-saving tip for scholastic memberships

Buying (or renewing) a scholastic (18-and-under) membership? Rose City Chess resells US Chess scholastic memberships at their bulk rate — $12.50 vs. $20 buying direct (as of this writing). It arrives as an e-voucher by email rather than an instant ID like buying direct from US Chess, so budget a little lead time before your first event. Adult memberships through them are at the regular price, so buying direct is simplest there.

2 Find tournaments nationwide

Every US Chess–rated tournament in the country is listed centrally, and it's searchable by location.

  • Upcoming Tournaments — filter by state, or search by address and a mile radius, to find events near you.
  • National Events Calendar — the marquee national and scholastic championships (state/regional qualifiers often feed into these).
  • Not sure which state body organizes events near you? US Chess State Chapters lists every state's affiliate.

3 Find tournaments in Oregon / Portland

Each state has its own US Chess–affiliated organization that runs local events — usually split between an adult/open federation and a scholastic (K-12) one. For Oregon:

  • Oregon Chess Federation (OCF) — the state's US Chess affiliate for adult and open events, including the annual Oregon Open and Oregon Closed championship.
  • Oregon Scholastic Chess Federation (OSCF) — K-12 events, including the OSCF state championships; see also their state tournaments page.

Several Portland-area clubs and academies also run their own regular, US Chess–rated tournaments directly:

  • Puddletown Chess — a 501(c)(3) nonprofit; runs monthly scholastic tournaments and free training classes.
  • Chess A2Z — nonprofit chess education and tournaments, co-organizes events like the Oregon Junior Open.
  • Rose City Chess — lessons, camps, and regular meetup tournaments around Portland.
  • Roshen's Chess Academy (Roshen Nair Foundation) — monthly scholastic tournaments run as fundraisers for OCF and Portland Chess Club.

Playing somewhere other than Oregon? Use the State Chapters link above to find your own state's equivalent — most states run the same adult/scholastic split.

4 Find a local scholastic chess club

Rather than a fixed list (club rosters and contact info change often — this guide won't try to keep pace with that), use the maintained directories below:

  • US Chess Club/Affiliate Directory — search by ZIP code and distance, filterable to schools and chess camps/programs specifically — this covers the whole country, not just Oregon.
  • In the Portland area: Portland Chess Club (est. 1914) runs regular play and keeps its own list of other Oregon-area clubs and organizations, scholastic included.
  • OSCF (linked in Step 3) is also a good source for which schools currently field a scholastic team, since school clubs form and dissolve year to year.

Once you're registered

A few of this site's own tools are useful from here:

  • Heading into an event and want to know what your rating will look like afterward? Try the USCF Rating Estimator (or the FIDE one, if it's FIDE-rated).
  • Organizing a school or club tournament yourself, rather than just playing in one? Swiss Pairings runs the whole event — pairings, standings, tiebreaks, and a wall display.
  • After your first few rated games, drop the PGNs into Performance Analysis for a full breakdown of where to focus training before the next one.
